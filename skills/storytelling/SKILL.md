# Storytelling

## Job

Build the structure that makes an audience want to know what comes next and understand the idea before it is named.

This is a narrative-structure skill, not a sentence-polishing skill.

Default rule:

`observation / tension → question → evidence / example → mechanism → insight → consequence`

not:

`framework name → definition → explanation → examples`

## Trigger

Use this skill when the user asks for things such as:

- tell this as a story
- improve the storytelling
- give this a stronger narrative arc
- turn these notes into a story
- make this article feel like one coherent talk
- turn this idea into a post, article, talk, or essay
- make the argument unfold naturally
- make this easier to understand through an example
- fix the opening or ending of this story
- make the insight land harder

If the user asks only for sentence-level wording changes, use a wordsmithing skill instead. Storytelling decides what happens when, what the audience knows at each point, and why they should keep going.

## 1. Lock the thesis

Before shaping the story, identify the governing idea in one sentence.

If the user has already accepted a thesis, hook, or framing, preserve it unless the user asks to change it or new evidence makes it untenable.

Classify new material as:

`proof | nuance | contradiction | candidate new thesis`

Default anecdotes, examples, metrics, and scenes to **proof**. A vivid story is not automatically the point of the piece.

Run the removal test:

> If removing this anecdote changes the intended thesis, has the story accidentally become the thesis?

If yes, either restore the original hierarchy or explicitly choose the new thesis.

## 2. Start with a live question

Strong stories create a question the audience wants answered.

Useful openings include:

- a concrete observation;
- an unexpected detail;
- a decision with consequences;
- a contradiction;
- a conventional assumption that is about to fail;
- a human puzzle;
- a before/after gap; or
- a tension between two things that both appear true.

Do not manufacture stakes, conflict, emotion, or controversy that the source does not support.

The opening does not need to explain the whole argument. It needs to make the next paragraph necessary.

## 3. Reveal the mechanism progressively

Do not dump the conclusion at the top and spend the rest of the piece defending it unless the genre requires that style.

For explanatory or idea-driven storytelling, prefer:

`observation → conventional frame → friction / exception → concrete example → mechanism → implication`

Let each section answer one question while creating the next.

The audience should feel the model becoming clearer, not feel that they are reading a framework checklist.

## 4. Show before name

When the idea includes a specialized term, framework, or original concept, make the audience understand the distinction first.

Use:

`make the mechanism legible → name it → show the consequence`

not:

`name it → define it → explain why it matters`

A term is strongest when it arrives as the natural name for something the audience already understands.

Avoid stopping the story for glossary-style definitions when the narrative itself can teach the meaning.

## 5. Make examples do work

A concrete example should expose the limit of the old frame, prove the new mechanism, or make an abstract idea intuitive.

Ask:

> If I remove the example, does the audience understand the reframe just as well?

If yes, the example may be decoration rather than evidence.

Use enough detail to make the example real, but do not let the example consume the argument unless the story itself is the subject.

Personal stories are evidence and illustration by default, not automatic protagonists.

## 6. Choose the genre before the structure

Do not treat every format as the same story at a different length.

### Short post

Lead quickly with one sharp observation, story beat, decision, or tension. Keep the conceptual load bounded. Use the story to land one idea and stop before the explanation becomes an essay.

### Article / essay

Default to a talk-like arc for original ideas:

`human observation or puzzle → question → progressive discovery → mechanism → examples / evidence → broader implication → ending that changes the opening`

Do not create an article by stretching a short post, adding headings, and repeating the same idea.

### Executive narrative

Make the decision and consequence legible quickly. Prefer:

`problem → why the obvious answer is insufficient → underlying mechanism → evidence → decision / implication`

Do not bury the business consequence beneath scene-setting.

### Personal / leadership story

Use chronology only where chronology creates meaning. The story should reveal a decision, tension, change in understanding, or consequence rather than merely recount events.

## 7. Use frameworks lightly

Structures such as STAR, PAS, BAB, AIDA, or other narrative frameworks can help organize material, but they are scaffolding rather than visible content.

Do not label the sections or force every story into the same template.

Choose the structure that best fits the source material.

If the framework is more visible than the idea, remove the framework.

## 8. Build transitions from causality

A strong transition answers:

- because of that, what changed?
- what did we realize next?
- what did the old explanation fail to explain?
- what new question appeared?
- what consequence follows from the mechanism?

Avoid transitions that merely announce sections such as “another important point” or “the next lesson is.”

The story should move because the reasoning moves.

## 9. Earn the ending

Do not force a call to action, question, moral, inspirational line, or summary.

A strong ending often does one of three things:

1. changes how the opening observation is understood;
2. states the decision or consequence created by the mechanism; or
3. compresses the governing insight into its clearest form after the audience has earned it.

The ending should feel inevitable in retrospect, not attached afterward.

## 10. Protect truth and voice

Storytelling may improve structure, pacing, emphasis, and sequence. It must not:

- invent experience;
- manufacture emotional stakes;
- strengthen unsupported claims;
- turn correlation into causation;
- erase uncertainty;
- convert a hypothesis into a result;
- exaggerate a conflict to make the story more dramatic; or
- replace the user's natural voice with generic content language.

When source evidence is incomplete, keep the gap visible.

## Final checks

Before returning the story, verify:

1. What is the one governing thesis?
2. Does the opening create a real question or tension?
3. Does each section move the audience toward the insight?
4. Are anecdotes serving the thesis rather than hijacking it?
5. Does every example perform explanatory or evidentiary work?
6. Is any specialized term shown before it is named?
7. Does the structure fit the genre?
8. Are the stakes and facts supported?
9. Does the ending change, complete, or sharpen the opening?
10. Could the story be simpler without losing the mechanism?

If a check fails, revise the structure before polishing the sentences.

## Default output

When the user asks for a finished story, return the finished artifact rather than explaining the method.

When the user asks for structure or diagnosis, provide the narrative spine first:

`Opening | Tension / question | Key beats | Mechanism / insight | Ending`

If the user requests alternatives, vary the **angle or narrative spine**, not just the wording.
