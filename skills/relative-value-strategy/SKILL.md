# Relative Value Strategy

## Purpose

Use this skill when the strategic question is not simply whether something is good, useful, differentiated, or technically impressive.

The governing question is:

> **What does this specific decision-maker value, what are the real alternatives, and where do we create enough relative advantage to change the decision?**

The method is designed to prevent a common strategy failure: starting from our capabilities and working backward until we invent a customer need that makes them look valuable.

Reason in this order:

`decision-maker → desired outcome → value drivers → importance → alternatives → relative performance → relative premium → human incentives → offering → priorities`

## When to use

Use for:

- product and portfolio strategy;
- positioning and differentiation;
- pricing and value capture;
- market entry;
- executive or career positioning;
- build / buy / partner decisions;
- deciding what not to fund;
- evaluating capability bundles; and
- pressure-testing a strategic thesis against the customer's actual choice.

## 1. Define the real decision

Identify:

- who is deciding;
- what decision they are making;
- what outcome they are trying to produce;
- what constraints materially shape the choice;
- what time horizon matters; and
- what resource is actually being allocated.

Do not use a broad market segment if the decision is account-specific, role-specific, or situation-specific.

## 2. Identify the real alternatives

List what the decision-maker can actually do instead.

Include when relevant:

- incumbent;
- direct competitor;
- adjacent substitute;
- internal build;
- workaround;
- two or more specialized vendors / teams / hires;
- delay;
- do nothing; or
- reallocate the resource elsewhere.

Do not compare only against the weakest convenient alternative.

## 3. Identify value drivers

Define the dimensions that materially change willingness to choose, pay, adopt, expand, hire, promote, fund, or allocate resources.

A value driver is customer value, not seller capability.

Examples:

- incremental revenue;
- customer experience;
- time to value;
- operating simplicity;
- reliability;
- control;
- risk reduction;
- learning speed;
- measurable business outcome;
- implementation burden;
- strategic flexibility;
- future option value.

If a capability does not change a decision-relevant outcome, it is not yet a value driver.

## 4. Weight importance

Not every value driver matters equally.

For this specific decision, classify each as:

`CRITICAL | HIGH | MEDIUM | LOW`

Use numeric weights only when the evidence supports them. Do not manufacture precision.

## 5. Compare relative performance

Evaluate the offering against the best plausible alternatives on each important driver.

Use:

`ADVANTAGE | PARITY | DISADVANTAGE | UNKNOWN`

Ask:

- Where are we clearly better?
- Where are we merely good?
- Where are alternatives already sufficient?
- Which disadvantages are fatal?
- Which advantages actually matter to this buyer?

Existing solutions should be allowed to shrink the thesis.

## 6. Locate the relative premium

Classify each important driver:

- **High importance + strong relative advantage** → likely premium
- **High importance + parity** → table stakes
- **Low importance + strong capability** → impressive, but strategically secondary
- **High importance + disadvantage** → structural gap or reason not to compete

The strategic crux is usually one or two value drivers, not a list of every strength.

If evidence proves relevance but not scarcity or superiority, say:

`RELEVANT / RELATIVE PREMIUM UNPROVEN`

## 7. Test the bundle

When the claimed value comes from a combination of capabilities, ask:

> **Could the buyer get essentially the same outcome by buying, hiring, or assembling the components separately?**

A bundle deserves premium only when integration itself changes value through materially better:

- coordination;
- speed;
- reliability;
- learning;
- accountability;
- economics; or
- outcome quality.

If two ordinary components produce the same result, do not invent a bundle premium.

## 8. Run the Elephant Check

Before accepting the economics, put the humans and institutions back into the model.

Core anti-pattern:

> **ELEPHANT ANTI-PATTERN:** A strategy assumes an actor will stop spending, surrender economics, abandon an incentive, cooperate, or behave “rationally” merely because the proposed system makes that behavior globally efficient.

Required question:

> **What will the human or institution actually do with the freed resource?**

For every material actor, identify:

- actual objective / incentive;
- baseline behavior;
- resource being saved, displaced, or constrained;
- likely resource disposition;
- second-order adaptation; and
- elasticity over the relevant time horizon.

Classify resource disposition as:

`eliminated | reallocated | delayed | accelerated | expanded | contracted | defended/withheld | unresolved`

### Advertising prior

Within an approved planning horizon, treat this as a defeasible prior:

> **Committed marketing budget is generally intended to be deployed. The primary economic decision is therefore allocation among alternatives, not spend versus no-spend, unless evidence establishes meaningful budget elasticity.**

Operationally:

`poor action ≠ unspent budget`

Usually:

`poor action → budget reallocated to another audience / channel / format / platform / period`

Always distinguish:

- spend reduction;
- spend reallocation; and
- budget expansion / contraction.

If the thesis only works because actors voluntarily surrender value or resources disappear, revise or kill it.

## 9. Define the offering

Translate the surviving relative advantage into a coherent offering.

State:

- target decision segment;
- customer outcome;
- lead value driver(s);
- relative advantage;
- proof required;
- product / service / role boundary;
- value-capture mechanism; and
- explicit non-priorities.

A coherent offering is not a catalog of everything available.

## 10. Choose value capture

Tie value capture to the actual value mechanism.

Depending on context, this may mean:

- price premium;
- usage pricing;
- outcome-linked pricing;
- share of value;
- more budget allocation;
- executive scope;
- strategic control of a product line;
- adoption priority; or
- resource priority.

Do not choose pricing or scope because it is fashionable. Choose it because it matches how value is created and how the buyer behaves.

## 11. Decide what not to prioritize

A strategy is incomplete until it creates non-priorities.

Explicitly identify:

- important drivers already adequately served by alternatives;
- features that do not change the decision;
- attractive markets where relative advantage is weak;
- customer segments where the offering is structurally disadvantaged; and
- investments that dilute the lead source of value.

## Evidence discipline

Separate:

- decision-maker evidence;
- offering evidence;
- alternative evidence;
- behavioral evidence; and
- inference.

Never infer customer value solely from seller strengths.

Never infer scarcity solely from relevance.

Never assume global efficiency creates incentive compatibility.

## Output

For a full diagnostic, return:

### Decision
One sentence defining the decision-maker and actual choice.

### Relative Value Map
`Value driver | Importance | Best alternative | Offering performance | Relative advantage | Evidence | Confidence`

### Strategic Crux
The one or two drivers where the decision is most likely won or lost.

### Elephant Check
`Actor | Actual incentive | Baseline behavior | Freed/displaced resource | Likely disposition | Adaptation | Elasticity`

### Offering
A compact proposition built around the strategic crux.

### Non-priorities
What should not lead and what should not receive resources.

### Evidence gaps
What must be verified before making a stronger claim.

### Verdict
End with one:

- `RELATIVE_PREMIUM_SUPPORTED`
- `RELEVANT_BUT_PREMIUM_UNPROVEN`
- `PARITY / TABLE_STAKES`
- `STRUCTURAL_DISADVANTAGE`
- `STRATEGY_FAILS_ELEPHANT`

## Final checks

Before finalizing:

1. Did we define the buyer's decision before evaluating our capabilities?
2. Did we include strong alternatives, including internal build and decomposition?
3. Do value drivers describe outcomes rather than features?
4. Did we distinguish importance from capability strength?
5. Did we separate relative advantage from simple relevance?
6. Did existing alternatives get permission to shrink the thesis?
7. Does any claimed bundle premium survive the decomposition test?
8. Did we model what actors actually do with saved or displaced resources?
9. Does the strategy survive second-order adaptation?
10. Did we create explicit non-priorities?

## Lineage and attribution

The value-strategy foundation is an independent operationalization of concepts attributed to Dwight Porter, including Value Drivers, Product Offering thinking, and relative customer value / value-capture logic.
